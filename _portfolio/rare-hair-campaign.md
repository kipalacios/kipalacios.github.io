overview:
  problem: >
    Millions of people with visual and mobility disabilities face daily barriers identifying,
    opening, and using standard beauty products. Accessibility research shows tactile markers and
    intuitive packaging significantly increase independence and safety for blind and low-vision
    users — yet few beauty brands prioritise meaningful accessibility beyond compliance. Inclusive
    hair care packaging is nearly nonexistent.
  goal: >
    Raise awareness of the accessibility gap in hair care, position Rare Beauty as a pioneer in
    disability-forward design, and drive adoption among both disabled and non-disabled consumers
    by making the case that usability and inclusivity are design strengths — not trade-offs
    against performance or luxury.
  audience_primary: >
    Blind and low-vision individuals; people with limited mobility or dexterity (arthritis,
    tremors, muscular disorders, fatigue conditions); disability advocates, accessibility experts,
    and inclusive-design communities; and existing Rare Beauty consumers.
  audience_secondary: >
    Gen Z and Millennial beauty consumers who value ethical and inclusive brands; beauty
    influencers focused on advocacy, transparency, and innovation; and retailers and industry
    leaders seeking accessible product offerings.

      - "Rare Beauty, founded by Selena Gomez, today unveiled Rare Hair, a new line of hair care products designed for blind, low-vision, and mobility-challenged users. The launch marks the brand's first expansion outside of cosmetics and reflects its ongoing commitment to building a more inclusive, user-centered beauty industry."
      - "With the release of Rare Hair, Rare Beauty aims to address a long-standing gap in the hair care market: the lack of packaging designed for people who experience barriers to grip, dexterity, or visual identification. The brand developed a tactile packaging system that enables users to distinguish products quickly and confidently through touch alone — raised spheres for shampoo, raised lines for conditioner, and a swirled pattern for styling products."
      - "Each bottle is equipped with an ergonomic pump, designed for effortless dispensing by users with limited hand strength or dexterity. The packaging system was created in consultation with accessibility advocates and product-design specialists, and involved extensive feedback from blind and low-vision individuals to ensure the line meets real, everyday accessibility needs."
      - "\"Beauty should be inclusive, and we want to make sure everyone can enjoy hair care products that are not only effective but also easy to use,\" said Selena Gomez, Rare Beauty founder. \"With Rare Hair, we're continuing our commitment to creating products that empower all users to feel confident and included.\""
      - "Rare Hair's formulas uphold the brand's existing values, featuring vegan, cruelty-free, high-performance ingredients tailored to different hair needs. The collection includes a hydrating shampoo, nourishing conditioner, and flexible styling cream — each created to deliver results while maintaining a sensorial experience that aligns with Rare Beauty's soft, calming aesthetic. The brand emphasised that accessibility should never come at the expense of performance or luxury."
      - "\"Rare Hair is about more than just hair products. It's about designing with empathy and understanding the needs of the people who have been underserved by the beauty industry,\" said Joyce Kim, Rare Beauty chief product officer."
      - "Rare Hair will be available beginning October 2025 at RareBeauty.com, Sephora, and select retail partners nationwide, in recognition of Blindness Awareness Month."
  - kind: Opinion Piece
    headline: "The Beauty Industry Has a Blind Spot. Rare Hair Fills It."
    doc: assets/docs/rare-hair/rare-hair-opinion-piece.docx
    doc_label: Download the opinion piece (DOCX)
    paragraphs:
      - "When a beauty brand like Rare Beauty proposes a hair care line with packaging deliberately designed for blind, low-vision, and mobility-challenged users, it does more than introduce a new product — it sends a message. It says that beauty, self-care, and everyday routines should not be privileges reserved for only some, and that design choices must reflect the lived experiences of an inclusive population."
      - "The reality is stark: globally, more than 1 billion people — about 15–20% of the population — live with some form of disability. In the United States alone, this demographic, along with their friends and families, wields substantial spending power. Yet mainstream consumer products too often ignore the needs of those who don't interact with the world in the \"average\" way. For people with vision loss or limited dexterity, simple tasks like distinguishing a shampoo from a conditioner can become frustrating barriers."
      - "Academic research confirms that inclusive packaging isn't just a nicety. In a study on cosmetic packaging and visually impaired persons, participants reported that accessible packaging made a real difference in their ability to select, use, and dispose of products without assistance. Inclusive design can reduce the stigma many visually impaired people face when they're forced to say \"I need help\" to do something private."
      - "There's also a compelling business case. Inclusive design doesn't just fulfil an ethical obligation or serve a niche market — it opens up a vast and underserved consumer base, building loyalty among consumers who often feel ignored by mainstream products."
      - "Moreover, inclusive design often benefits all users. This is the essence of what scholars call the curb-cut effect: accommodations created for people with disabilities frequently end up helping many others. A tactile-packaged shampoo bottle with an ergonomic pump doesn't only help someone with visual needs; it helps anyone who wants a quick, spill-free routine."
      - "In a world where beauty standards have too often equated \"normal\" with \"able-bodied,\" Rare Hair would stand as a counter-narrative — signalling that inclusivity isn't about superficial representation but about real, tactile, everyday usability. It would challenge other beauty brands to ask: who are we designing for — only idealised bodies, or all bodies?"

  faqs:
    - q: What is Rare Hair?
      a: "An accessible hair care line designed for blind, low-vision, and mobility-challenged users. The collection features tactile ridged bottles identifiable by touch and ergonomic pumps for easier use: Hydrating Shampoo (vertical ridges), Nourishing Conditioner (horizontal ridges), and Styling Cream (diagonal ridges). All formulas are vegan, cruelty-free, sulfate- and paraben-free, and suitable for all hair types."
    - q: How does Rare Hair reflect Rare Beauty's mission?
      a: "It extends the brand's core mission of inclusivity beyond makeup. Just as Rare Beauty's original cosmetics were designed with easy-to-grip caps for users with dexterity challenges, Rare Hair's tactile and ergonomic design ensures hair care can be navigated by touch alone."
    - q: Why did Rare Beauty create Rare Hair?
      a: "Founder Selena Gomez and the Rare Beauty team recognised a gap in the hair care market for people with disabilities or vision impairments, and developed the line to meet those needs while maintaining the brand's commitment to innovation and emotional connection."
    - q: When and where will it be available?
      a: "The collection launches September 2025 in the United States — with select international rollouts planned for early 2026 — via RareBeauty.com, Sephora, and select retail partners."
