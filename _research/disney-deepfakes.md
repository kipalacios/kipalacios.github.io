brief:
  context: >
    AI now makes de-aging, digital doubles, and the recreation of beloved characters cheap and
    accessible, and it's showing up across film, television, and advertising. For most studios
    that's a production question. For Disney — a brand whose entire value rests on emotion,
    nostalgia, and human storytelling — it's a reputation question.
  purpose: >
    To measure how Disney's target audience responds to the company's use of AI and deepfakes
    in storytelling and advertising: what it does to trust, which uses feel acceptable versus
    unethical, and what audiences expect from Disney on disclosure and consent.

research_questions:
  - How familiar are audiences with deepfake technology, and can they identify one when shown an example?
  - Are reactions to AI and deepfakes in Disney media positive, negative, or neutral?
  - To what extent should Disney disclose AI use — especially with legacy characters or deceased actors?
  - Where do audiences draw the ethical line on consent, authenticity, and manipulation?
  - How does AI use affect trust in Disney's reputation for authentic, emotionally resonant storytelling?
  - Are attitudes shaped by age, familiarity with Disney content, or prior exposure to AI media?

hypotheses:
  - AI and deepfakes offer real creative and operational benefits, which is why they're spreading through entertainment companies like Disney.
  - Undisclosed deepfakes trigger negative responses around authenticity, manipulation, and ethics.
  - Audiences accept minor enhancements, like de-aging or background refinement, far more readily than full character replacement.
  - Audiences expect Disney to disclose AI use, and a lack of transparency reduces brand trust.
  - Younger audiences show less concern about AI than older or family-oriented viewers.

method:
  summary: >
    An online survey distributed by text, email, and social media, open for one week and taking
    about eight minutes. Respondents were given a definition of deepfakes up front to set a shared
    baseline, then shown a real Disney deepfake beside the unaltered actor and asked to identify
    it, before moving on to ethics, trust, and advertising.
  sample: >
    46 U.S. adults aged 18–34 who regularly consume Disney content, recruited through convenience
    sampling. The sample split evenly between 18–24 and 25–34; 57% identified as female, and 73%
    held a bachelor's degree or higher.
  formats:
    - Likert scales
    - Semantic differential
    - Ratio
    - Open-ended
  dimensions:
    - name: Cognitive
      desc: Knowledge and awareness of AI and deepfakes
    - name: Affective
      desc: Attitudes toward AI, including trust and authenticity
    - name: Evaluative
      desc: Ethical judgements, especially about replicating a likeness
    - name: Behavioral
      desc: Future intentions, support, and likelihood of engaging

recommendations:
  - title: Disclose, and explain
    body: >
      Be upfront when AI or deepfakes are used, and show how they support the storytelling rather
      than replace it. Transparency builds the AI literacy that was missing, and removes the sense
      of being quietly deceived.
  - title: Use AI as a tool, not a replacement
    body: >
      Keep AI to minor, practical work — touch-ups, de-aging, localization. Replacing an actor, or
      making a deepfake a main character, read to respondents as "creatively bankrupt."
  - title: Respect the legacy
    body: >
      When recreating a deceased actor, secure consent from the estate, share that consent with
      audiences, and hold the result to a high quality bar to avoid an uncanny, disrespectful end
      product.

limitations:
  - Convenience sampling drew mostly USC students and the team's extended networks, so the results aren't generalizable.
  - The media examples didn't produce much emotional activation — more moving examples would test the brand connection more fully.
  - The reasons behind indifferent responses weren't captured, which would have clarified whether the neutrality was genuine or an artifact of survey fatigue.
